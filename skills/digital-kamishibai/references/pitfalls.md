# Pitfalls: mistakes families have made more than once

Each entry: what happens, why, and what to do. The `plan` / `script` tag says when it bites.
Most of these were found only after the work was finished — which is why they are worth knowing before.

## Too long, found out too late

- **What happens**: the script is written, then found to be far too long, and cut down after the fact.
  It happens with almost every work.
- **Why**: there was no number to stop at. "About 30 minutes" does not stop a writer.
  And adding up changes edit by edit drifts away from what is really there.
- **What to do**:
  1. Put numbers in the plan: total length (about 30 minutes **including videos**), slides, a word or character
     target, and **a ceiling per slide**. Spare time is not filled.
  2. Use the per-slide numbers as **ceilings**, not quotas: they stop the cutting from removing what the youngest
     listener needs to follow.
  3. After editing, **count again** rather than adding up the changes.
  4. A slide whose music must play to its end takes as long as the music. Reading faster does not shorten it.
  5. Reading speed in a real show (pauses, slide changes) is slower than reading one slide aloud. Words per minute are
     for estimating the total, never for judging a narrator: what makes narration sound rushed is missing pauses and a
     flat voice, not speed.

## Things the ear cannot take in

- **What happens**: slide numbers, internal labels or file names end up in the narration and are read aloud.
- **Why**: the script holds two kinds of text — narration the narrator **says**, and notes the makers **read**.
  Side by side on one screen, the writer forgets which is which.
- **What to do**: **put nothing in the narration that cannot be understood by ear, then and there.**
  Not "slide 12", not "act two", not "in these nineteen slides", not a file name.
  Refer to another work by a name that means something when heard ("the story about the lighthouse").

## Production notes leaking to the narrator

- **What happens**: the narrator's screen shows lines that were never meant to be read — a reminder to replace
  something, the reason for a design choice.
- **Why**: one mark was doing two jobs: `>` lines are shown to the narrator, and notes written with `>` were shown too.
- **What to do**: notes for the makers go in `<!-- ... -->`. `>` is only for what the narrator should see.

## Too many questions to the audience

- **What happens**: questions to the children are scattered through the narration, then cut just before or after the show.
- **Why**: a question looks good on the page but **stops the narration** in the room. Nobody counted them.
- **What to do**: set a maximum in the plan, with where each goes and who answers. Write them as free cues (`>`),
  not as narration. **Experiments and hands-on demonstrations go after the show**, all in one place.

## Facts checked after writing

- **What happens**: a wrong fact (history, science) is found after the work is finished, and the script, the pictures and
  everything built from them are remade.
- **Why**: facts were checked **while writing**. Then you look for facts that fit the sentence you already wrote.
- **What to do**: a **fact gate** in the plan — list the facts, check them, and only then write the script.
  The gate does more than fix errors: sometimes it changes what a slide is about.
  - "This is one researcher's view, not a proven law" is a fact too. Keep it, and keep the wording inside it.
  - Check the **joins**, not only the points: with "four years later", write out both years and subtract;
    with a total ("three tries in ten years"), check every part of the total is told; with family relations or order of
    birth ("the second daughter"), line up the birth years. Cheapest of all: leave out what the story does not need.
  - Check **the words on the screen against the narration**. When they disagree, sources decide which is right.
  - A fact found wrong after the show is still fixed, and everything handed out is rebuilt — otherwise the next
    performance repeats the error.

## Earlier works: invented links, or links only pointed at

- **What happens**: a work "pays off" a hint from an earlier work that the earlier work never contained. Or it explains
  something again in new words, so there are two versions. Or it only points ("remember from last time?") and the
  children have forgotten, so nothing after it makes sense.
- **Why**: nobody keeps a list of what earlier works said. Writing from memory pays off the work in the writer's head.
- **What to do**: keep a short list of what each work introduced (ideas, comparisons, hints). **Open the earlier work
  and find the hint before paying it off.** When an earlier idea is needed again, **say it again, in the words used
  the first time** — briefly, inside an existing slide. Do not re-teach it in new words, and do not only point at it.

## No designed emotional curve

- **What happens**: near the end, the middle turns out to drag, and slides and music are rebuilt.
- **Why**: the work was ordered by information only. Nothing was planned for feeling — hook, peaks, dips, climax, afterglow.
- **What to do**:
  1. Put the curve in the plan: which slides are the hook, the peaks, the dips, the climax and the afterglow.
  2. No stretch of more than about ten minutes without an event (a video, a change of music, a peak, a drop).
     If there is one, rework the peaks and dips; **merge similar slides before adding new ones**.
  3. End heavy moments in the afterglow: no explanation, no punch line.
  4. **Count the weight**, not only where it sits: how many deaths or losses, how many minor-key songs, how many dips in a
     row. A plan with a fine curve can still be far too dark in total.
  5. **Say where the climax is.** A work that fades from sound to words to silence makes silence its peak.
     Darkness is better offset by a longer stretch that burns bright than by removing the dark.

## A negative plants what it denies

- **What happens**: a sentence meant to reassure ("there is nothing wrong with you") plants the very word it denies.
- **Why**: a negative first puts the denied thing on the table. The ear keeps what was put there. The writer feels it
  has been cancelled and cannot hear it.
- **What to do**:
  1. Do not deny the premise; **make it not arise** ("everyone has only a few close friends" instead of "having few
     friends is not a problem").
  2. If **the last line of a slide** or **words on the screen** contain "not", try once to say it in the positive.
     Last words and long-shown words stay longest.
  3. Exception: denying a thought the listener already says to themselves can free them ("it is hard, not because you
     are weak"). The question is whether the listener is already saying those words.

## Hype instead of a heading

- **What happens**: "Now comes the most important part" — and nothing about what comes.
- **Why**: the writer feels it matters and writes the feeling. The listener learns nothing, and braces.
- **What to do**: put a **heading**, not hype: what comes next, in a few words ("Why does climbing over a wall make you
  stronger?"). Often the next line already says it; then delete the preface.
  - **Shorten words, never steps of a cause.** "Hitting a wall makes you stronger" is false; *getting over* it does.
    Read the shortened sentence alone and ask: is this true?

## Pointing the lesson at one child

- **What happens**: on the slide meant for one particular child, the narration names that child.
- **Why**: the writer wants to be sure it reaches them. But **pointed at, a child hears an explanation; not pointed at,
  the child makes a discovery.** Nothing breaks when this goes wrong — the effect just quietly disappears.
- **What to do**:
  1. Save naming for a slide of personal messages (recognition, not a lesson).
  2. Do not deny a premise about the child (see above).
  3. **No singular "you"** in the room: it singles someone out. Use the plural.
  4. Something a child is practising is **played or shown**, not talked about.
  Whether something singles a child out depends on who is in the room, so no tool can check it: read those slides once
  with the room in mind.

## Trusting the picture to show what the narration says

- **What happens**: the narration says "three bends"; the picture shows one. Nobody notices until the show — and the
  listeners believe the picture.
- **Why**: a generated picture does not keep to the numbers and shapes in its prompt (numbers especially).
  Every check looked at text; nobody looked at the picture. Writing it in the prompt was taken as drawing it.
- **What to do**:
  1. For each slide where the narration **asserts something about the picture** (a number, a shape, a contrast),
     check exactly that, by eye, right after the picture is made.
  2. **Do not leave numbers to the picture generator.** If it misses twice, draw that part another way.
  3. What counts is the picture **as seen**, not as prompted. Write down what it actually shows.

## Walking on eggshells

- **What happens**: a subject touches something tender for one child (a low score, a weak point, a hard time), so
  the plan grows a list of things not to mention, then another, and the work turns timid. It loses what made it worth
  watching.
- **Why**: each caution looks harmless on its own. Nobody counts them; an AI helping with the plan tends to add more.
- **What to do**: draw **one line** — the one that really matters (for example: that child's own answers and the
  family's story about them are not told on stage). Everything else is told straight, as science or as a story.
  When "do not touch" items start to multiply, take it as the sign of too much care, not too little.
  What a parent wants to reach one child indirectly travels best in the parent's own first-person story
  (`pattern-language.md`), not in more cautions.

## Music

- **Write the music cue at the top of every slide** — "continues" and "none" too. A slide with no cue leaves the
  narrator guessing whether the music should be playing.
- **No music under a video** that has its own sound. Stop it before the video; decide whether it comes back after.
- **Songs with lyrics only where there is little narration.** Under narration, use an instrumental version.
  If a song with lyrics must be on a slide with narration, start it after the narration ends.
- **At a farewell, play the song of the one who receives** — the one who takes over, not the one who leaves.
- Check each file before use: silence at the start, applause or a second song at the end.
- Look at the songs together, not one by one: is the whole work bright or dark? A song's title does not tell you that
  it is used for mourning; look it up.

## A stray character from another script

- **What happens**: one character from another writing system turns up inside a word of the narration.
- **Why**: text written with an AI's help can carry one, and no check looks at the kind of characters.
- **What to do**: **read the whole script aloud once before the show.** It is also the narrator's rehearsal.
