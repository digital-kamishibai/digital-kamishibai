# Learning science: a 12-item check for the script

What kind of adult a child becomes — or what anyone carries away from a show — cannot be traced back to a kamishibai:
years of other causes pile up, and there is no second life to compare with. So the reasoning runs the other way: **not "this show is proven to work", but
"this show does not go against what is well established"** — and borrowing from what is.

### Three rules for using this

1. **Check the design; never measure the listeners.** A kamishibai works because it is a gift. Put a test or a
   questionnaire before or after the show and the gift becomes an intervention, and the listeners become subjects —
   children or adults. Observe only without intruding: things the listeners bring up by themselves later, what they choose to do,
   what was said anyway.
2. **Use it once, when the script is done.** Go through the twelve items below. Do not make a new meeting or ritual of it.
3. **Let the shows update it.** When experience rediscovers a principle, add it to Part A. When experience and science
   disagree, write it in Part C and see at home which holds — that disagreement is where something new is learned.

## Part A: where practice and science arrived at the same place

| Rule learned from practice | Established finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pictures and narration share the work: the ear gets the narration, the eye gets the picture. Words on the screen are not the narration written out | Redundancy and modality principles: on-screen text repeating the narration hinders learning; picture plus voice works best | Mayer, *Multimedia Learning* (2001; 3rd ed. 2021) |
| Write as if talking to the listeners, not as a report | Personalization principle: conversational style beats formal style | same |
| Children narrate the show | Learning by teaching; retrieval practice | Fiorella & Mayer (2013) *Contemp. Educ. Psychol.*; Roediger & Karpicke (2006) *Psych. Science* |
| Regular shows; series that come back to a theme | Spacing effect: spaced repetition beats massed practice | Cepeda et al. (2006) *Psych. Bulletin* |
| Carry it in a story; end heavy moments without explanation or punch line | Stories are a privileged form of memory; the afterglow lets the listener keep thinking | Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?* (2009) |
| The maker's true story, failures included, told in the first person | Family stories (studied with children as listeners): children who know their family's history — including setbacks ("oscillating" narratives) — show higher self-esteem and sense of control | Duke, Lazarus & Fivush (2008) *Psychotherapy* 45(2), 268–272 |
| Praise the way of practising, not talent | Process praise leads to choosing challenges; ability praise to avoiding them | Mueller & Dweck (1998) *JPSP* |
| Set the listeners' own things into the story (a piece a child practises, a parent's detour) | Utility value and self-relevance: connecting material to one's own life raises interest and results, most for the less confident | Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009) *Science* 326, 1410–1412 |
| Plant a question in one work and answer it in a later one | Information-gap theory of curiosity: a noticed gap drives exploration | Loewenstein (1994) *Psych. Bulletin* |

## Part B: frontier — in the science, not yet in practice

Candidates. When reviewing a script, ask whether there is a place for them. If adopted, move them to Part A.

- **B1. Self-explanation** (Chi et al. 1994). After the show, do not ask for impressions; ask only
  "**Which part did you like best?**" If the listener starts giving reasons, that is the self-explanation.
  Do not demand it; do not correct it.
- **B2. The listener makes the connection.** In Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009) the effect came from the students
  themselves writing how the material connected to their lives. Is there one place, in the story or after it, where a
  listener could say how this connects to them — without stopping the narration?
- **B3. Prequestions.** A question the listeners cannot yet answer, asked before the content, improves what they take
  from it. Does the opening ask a question the story answers near the end?
- **B4. The four phases of interest** (Hidi & Renninger 2006): triggered, maintained, emerging individual,
  well-developed. Surprise and story light it; choice and depth feed it later. Which phase is this work for?
  A subject a listener asked for is a sign of the later phases.
- **B5. Desirable difficulties** (Bjork 1994). When an earlier work matters, let the listeners **recall** it
  ("what happened to … ?" — a pause — then go on) rather than re-teaching it.

## Part C: friction — where science warns current practice

Read these every time; they set expectations.

- **C1. Seductive details** (Harp & Mayer 1998). Interesting but unrelated details — anecdotes, music, decoration — can
  **hurt** learning. Music, sound effects and jokes can be exactly that. They are tamed when they are **connected** to
  the theme (a piece whose structure mirrors the story's). Any element that is "just fun" is a candidate to cut.
  Open: in a family celebration, music is also a ritual that makes the listeners look forward to the next show; whether
  it hurts is watched for in what they bring up later.
- **C2. Mindset interventions have small effects** (Sisk et al. 2018: about 1% of variance in grades; intervention
  effect d = 0.08, somewhat larger for students at risk). Do not expect much from teaching a growth mindset. The main
  roads are family stories and lighting interest. A mindset is better **shown** in the maker's own failure than taught.
- **C3. Far transfer is hardly found** (Sala & Gobet 2017). Do not promise or expect "science stories make you
  smarter". What can reasonably be expected: interest in the subject, knowledge of it, and a shared family story.

## The check (once, when the script is done)

Not every item has to be Yes. **If you can say why it is No, it passes.**

- [ ] 1. Do pictures and narration share the work (no on-screen text repeating the narration; the narration is for the ear)? *(A: redundancy)*
- [ ] 2. Is it written as talking to the listeners? *(A: personalization)*
- [ ] 3. Are the listeners' own things set into it (holes filled — or left empty on purpose)? *(A: utility value; family stories, when the listeners are children)*
- [ ] 4. Is there at least one failure or detour of the maker's own, in the first person (when an adult is the maker)? *(A: family stories)*
- [ ] 5. Does the opening ask a question the story answers near the end? *(B3: prequestions)*
- [ ] 6. Is there one place where a listener could say how this connects to them? *(B2)*
- [ ] 7. Can you say which phase of interest this work is for? *(B4)*
- [ ] 8. Do references to earlier works make the listeners recall, rather than re-explain? *(B5)*
- [ ] 9. Is every effect (music, sounds, jokes, videos) connected to the theme? Is anything "just fun"? *(C1)*
- [ ] 10. Is there a slide that preaches a mindset or a moral? *(C2)*
- [ ] 11. Does the work promise to make anyone smarter? *(C3)*
- [ ] 12. After the show, will the listeners have room to choose and talk, without the maker filling it with explanation? *(B1; observe without intruding)*
